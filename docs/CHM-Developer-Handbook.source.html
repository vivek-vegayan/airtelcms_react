A note on accuracy and drift

Two things in CHM move faster than any document can. The first is the set of stored procedures in the live MySQL instance, which has been hand-edited on the server in the past and can differ from the .sql files in the repository. The second is the RBAC grant data, which administrators change through the UI at runtime. Treat the names and shapes in this handbook as a reliable map of the code, and always verify the live database before you change a procedure.

Chapter One
01

What CHM Is

CHM is the system Airtel's network operations teams use to take a proposed change to live network infrastructure from the moment it is raised to the moment it is closed — and to staff the engineers who do the work.

The business problem

A telecom operator's network is changed constantly: a card is swapped, a software version is upgraded, a link is re-homed, a configuration is pushed. Every one of those changes carries risk — of an outage, of a service-affecting window at the wrong hour, of two teams touching the same equipment at the same time. Airtel controls that risk with a formal change-management process, and CHM is the software that runs it.

Two things happen in CHM, and understanding that they are two things is the single most useful mental model a new joiner can have:

The two halves of CHM
Change governance A change request (a CRQ) arrives from Airtel's Remedy/Helix ITSM system. CHM walks it through seven controlled stages — validation, impact analysis, method-of-procedure authoring and review, scheduling approval, execution and closure — recording who did what, when, and with what outcome at every step.
Workforce scheduling The same engineers who execute those changes work shifts. CHM generates and publishes monthly rosters, handles shift swaps, leave and attendance, and knows who is on duty at the hour a change is scheduled to run.
↓ the two meet here ↓
Scheduling a change against a real roster A CRQ cannot be scheduled into a slot where the network is frozen, or where nobody with the right skills is rostered on. That constraint is why one product contains both halves rather than two products exchanging spreadsheets.

Who uses it

The roles below are the ones the code names. They are not job titles — they are RBAC role codes, and what a person sees is decided entirely by the modules granted to their role (Chapter 13).

Role codeWhat they do in CHM
TEAM_MEMBERAn engineer. Sees their own roster and leave, works the stages of CRQs assigned to them, responds to inbox requests.
TEAM_LEADRuns a team. Approves swaps and leave, assigns work, sees the team's roster.
DOMAIN_HEAD, SUB_DOMAIN_HEADOwn a slice of the organisation hierarchy. See every CRQ and every roster inside their domain or sub-domain.
FUNCTION_HEAD, VERTICAL_HEADHigher levels of the same hierarchy, with correspondingly broader visibility.
CAB rolesThe Change Advisory Board — approvers who accept, reject, delegate or reschedule a change on behalf of the services it will affect.
SUPER_ADMINConfigures the product itself: roles, modules, permissions, organisation hierarchy, notification rules. Also the only role that can read the audit log.

A working day, end to end

Following one change through the system is the fastest way to see how the modules relate.

  1. A CRQ is raised in Remedy/Helix and lands in CHM (the platform also exposes an inbound endpoint, /CHMDataAdapter/CreateCrq, for that integration).
  2. A planner opens Scheduler › CRQ Workflow and works the CRQ through Plan & Inventory: checking the plan, the inventory it touches and the tasks under it, and validating it.
  3. Impact Analysis runs — partly a script on a remote host that produces a CSV of impacted circuits, partly a human judgement recorded in the UI.
  4. A MOP (method of procedure) is created, uploaded as a PDF, then reviewed by somebody else in MOP Validate, who raises findings and either validates or rejects it.
  5. The change goes to the CAB for approval. Each affected service has an approver; they approve, reject, delegate, or ask for a reschedule.
  6. Approved, the CRQ is scheduled into a slot — checked against network freeze windows, holidays, and the roster of who is actually on duty.
  7. On the night, Activity Implement tracks execution ring by ring; Closure records the outcome and pushes status back to Remedy and to Cygnet.
  8. Every state change along the way can raise a notification into somebody's Inbox, and every state-changing action by a user is written to the audit log.
Why this matters for your code

Almost every screen in CHM is a view onto one of two record sets: a CRQ and its stage history, or a roster and its assignments. When you are lost in an unfamiliar feature, ask which of the two it is reading, and the data flow usually becomes obvious.

What CHM is not

  • Not the system of record for the change itself. Remedy/Helix is. CHM enriches, schedules and governs; it pushes status back.
  • Not an ORM application. There is no JPA, no Hibernate, no entity graph. All data access is stored procedures called through JdbcTemplate (Chapter 10).
  • Not a microservice estate. One Spring Boot deployable, one React bundle, one reverse proxy.

Glossary of the Domain

CHM's vocabulary is telecom-operations vocabulary. You will not guess these from the code, and the code assumes you know them. This is the chapter to dog-ear.

Change management

Platform vocabulary

TermMeaning

The JDK trap — read this before your first build

The back end must be built and run on JDK 17. On a newer JDK (25, for example) Lombok's annotation processing silently stops generating getters and setters, and the compiler then reports hundreds of “cannot find symbol” errors across roughly a hundred @Data-annotated classes. The errors point at your model classes and look like the code is broken; it is not.

Set JAVA_HOME to a JDK 17 installation and confirm with java -version before you conclude anything is wrong with the repository. In IntelliJ, check both Project SDK and the Maven runner JDK.

Clone the repositories

D:\CHM_airtel_scheduler\Github_Repo\
├── CHM_airtel_beta\      # front end (this is also where deployment/ lives)
└── airtelmanagement\     # back end (this is also where db/migration/ lives)

Keep the layout above. Several team scripts, and every path in this handbook, assume it.

Database access

CHM does not create its own schema on startup — there is no Flyway, no Liquibase and no Hibernate DDL. You connect to an existing MySQL instance with the CHM schemas already loaded. Ask the team for:

  • The host, user and password for the two schemas (user management and the CHM domain schema).
  • Whether you are pointing at a shared development database or a local copy. Most developers share one, which is why editing a stored procedure affects everybody — see Chapter 10.

Verify before you go further

  • java -version reports 17.
  • node -v reports 20 or 22.
  • You can connect to MySQL and run SHOW PROCEDURE STATUS WHERE Db = '<chm schema>'; and see a long list. If that list is short or empty, you are pointed at the wrong schema.

Running the Back End

The service reads its database credentials, SSH hosts and SFTP paths from a file outside the repository. Without that file it will not start, and the failure is not obvious. Create it first.

Step 1 — the external configuration file

AppPropertiesConfig is constructed before anything else needs a data source. In its constructor it picks a path based on the operating system, opens it, and reads every key it needs:

OSPath (hard-coded, not overridable)
WindowsC:\vegayan\simplus\airtelcms-config.properties
Linux/home/vegayan/simplus/config_airtel.properties

A template ships in the front-end repository at deployment/config/airtelcms-config.sample.properties. Copy it to the path above and fill it in. The keys fall into four groups:

Step 3 — run it

In development the team runs the application from IntelliJ, using the AirtelmanagementApplication main class. From the command line:

cd D:\CHM_airtel_scheduler\Github_Repo\airtelmanagement
mvnw.cmd clean package          # builds target\changemanagement-test.war
java -jar target\changemanagement-test.war

The WAR is executable — spring-boot-starter-tomcat is declared provided in the POM but the Spring Boot Maven plugin repackages it with an embedded container, and the deployment guides run it with plain java -jar.

Confirm it is up

curl http://127.0.0.1:8686/actuator/health
curl http://127.0.0.1:1857/test/hello

/test/** is a public endpoint (no token required), which makes /test/hello the cheapest possible reachability check.

The dev back end does not hot-reload

There is no dev-tools restart configured. After changing back-end code you must stop and restart the application in IntelliJ — a surprising number of “my endpoint returns 404” reports end here.

Logs

Logging is configured in logback-spring.xml. Two things are worth knowing on day one:

  • Both JdbcTemplates are LoggingJdbcTemplates, so every SQL statement is logged as a runnable line with its arguments already substituted, prefixed DB1 or DB2. When a screen shows the wrong data, copy the logged CALL into a MySQL client and run it — that single habit resolves most data bugs.
  • Several named loggers exist for noisy flows: Cancel_Crq_Logger, Submit_Plan_External_Logger, CRQ_Update_To_Chm and Schedule_Reschedule_Crq. They are declared on BaseService, so any service can write to them.
  • The log directory is D:/CHM_LOGS on Windows. On Linux this is overridden at launch by pointing --logging.config at deployment/config/logback-linux.xml.

CORS in development

If you run the back end on your own machine and the front end on the dev server, you are on two origins. The allowed origins are a hard-coded list in SecurityConfig.corsConfigurationSource() — a set of developer LAN addresses on ports 5173–5178 plus the production host. If your machine's address and port are not in that list, every request fails in the browser with a CORS error while working fine in curl. Add your origin to the list (and mention it in your PR — the list is long precisely because everybody does this).

What you will see build-wise

Two Vite settings in vite.config.ts exist to solve real bugs and are explained fully in Chapter 24; know that they are deliberate:

  • A manualChunks function puts nearly all of node_modules into one vendor chunk, with pdfjs split out. This is a request-count fix: the default splitting produced 146 JS files, many under 300 bytes, and the burst of requests tripped rate limiting.
Chapter Seven
07

Your First Login

CHM allows exactly one live session per account. That single design decision explains most of the confusion new joiners hit in their first hour.

How signing in works

  1. You enter an OLM ID and password. The front end posts them to POST /auth/v1/signin.
  2. The back end looks the username up in AUTH_CREDENTIAL, checks the password, and then checks whether that account already has a live session.
  3. On success it mints a JWT whose subject is the numeric user id and whose jti is a fresh UUID, writes a row to AUTH_JWT_TOKENS, returns the token as accessToken, and also sets it as an HttpOnly cookie named jwt.
  4. The front end stores the token, then calls GET /users/v2/getloggeduserdetails to fetch the profile and the full module → sub-module → permission tree that drives the whole UI.
  5. You land on the first route your permissions allow — there is no fixed home page.

The three failure messages

MessageHTTPMeaning and fix
User Not Found404No such OLM ID. Note that accounts do get deleted from the shared dev database — an ID that worked last month may not exist today.
Invalid Password401Wrong password. A master password configured in auth.master.password works for any account in non-production environments.
Already Logged403That account has a live session — possibly yours, in another browser or on another machine, possibly a colleague's. See below.
“Already Logged” and how to clear it properly

The login screen offers to terminate the stranded session. That calls POST /auth/v1/session/terminate, which is deliberately reachable without a token — you cannot get one while the old session holds the slot — but re-checks the account password before ending anything. This replaced an older design where posting a bare { "olmId": "..." } to the logout route would end anyone's session if you knew their ID.

The blunt alternative, when you have database access, is to delete the account's row from AUTH_JWT_TOKENS. That is the same thing the endpoint does.

Session behaviour you should expect

  • Refreshing the page does not log you out. The token lives in localStorage, and on every cold start AuthHydrator re-validates it against the back end before trusting it.
  • Opening a second tab works. localStorage is shared across tabs, and a storage event keeps them in sync — sign out in one tab and every other tab signs out too.
  • “Invalid session” on every request means the AUTH_JWT_TOKENS row is gone or expired. It never means the JWT itself was malformed. See Chapter 24.
  • A 401 anywhere tears the session down deliberately. The RTK Query base query clears storage, dispatches logout, resets the entire query cache and shows a toast — so the next user to sign in on that machine can never see the previous one's cached data.

What to click first

Once in, spend twenty minutes doing this, in order. It maps the product onto the chapters ahead faster than reading does:

  1. Scheduler › CRQ Workflow. Open a CRQ. Look at the stage rail across the top — that is the seven-stage model of Chapter 15 rendered directly.
  2. Scheduler › CRQ Journey. The same CRQ as a pipeline. Note that this view is shared with the CAB module.
  3. Roster › Roster View. A month of shifts for a sub-domain. Click the small i on a cell for the daily assignment detail.
  4. My Dashboard. Your own attendance, roster, leave and notification preferences — the merged Home + Me workspace.
  5. Global Settings › Admin Settings (if your role can see it). This is the RBAC editor. Everything you change here changes what other people's sidebars look like.
If the sidebar is nearly empty

That is RBAC working, not a bug. Sidebar items are driven entirely by the modules granted to your role. Ask for a role with broader grants, or read Chapter 13 to see exactly which module name each item requires.

Design points worth copying when you add auditing to a new endpoint:

  • The aspect advises @AfterReturning, so a method that threw records nothing.
  • It also inspects the return value and skips the write for a non-2xx ResponseEntity or an ApiResponse whose status is Fail or Error. CHM has several endpoints that report refusal with HTTP 200; an audit trail claiming those succeeded would be worse than none.

  • Login is audited explicitly rather than by annotation, because at that moment the security context is still empty and the aspect could not resolve an actor.
  • Only super admins can read the log. That check is enforced in the back end independently of the two front-end guards.

The Database Contract

Stored procedures are the interface between Java and the data. Feature code does not write SELECT. This is the single most important convention in the codebase, and it comes with rules that are easy to break silently.

The rule

From the migration README, verbatim in spirit: stored procedures are the interface. The Java layer calls CALL <proc>(…) through DatabaseUtils; feature code does not write SELECT/INSERT/UPDATE text. A new read path means a new procedure, not new SQL in a service.

The handful of raw queries that do exist are infrastructure, not features — the session lookup in TokenValidationService, the authority join in UserPermissionService, the credential lookup in AuthService.

Calling a procedure

DatabaseUtils offers a method per result shape. Choosing the right one is most of the job:

MethodUse when
executeProcedureAndFetchObjects(V1)A single result set mapped to a DTO list via BeanPropertyRowMapper. The everyday read.
executeProcedureGetDataWithErrorSame, but the procedure may report a failure as an error_message column. Throws DatabaseOperationException if it sees one.
executeProcedureSingleResultWithErrorThe above, returning the first row or null.
fetchOrderedKeyValueFromProcedureColumn order matters and the shape is dynamic — returns LinkedHashMap rows.
executeProcedureAndProvideKeyValueWithHeadersFormatThe same, packaged as { headers, data } for a generic table UI.
updateUsingProcedureA write whose only outcome you need is success or failure. See the warning below.
executeProcedureForMessage / …V1A write whose procedure returns a status/message row. V1 returns ApiResponse.
executeProcedureAllResultSets / …LastResultSetA procedure that emits several result sets. Several CHM procedures do.
extractMultiPagedResultA paged read: returns ProcedurePageResult<T> carrying totalCount and data.
Trap 1 — updateUsingProcedure reports success for a rollback

If a procedure rolls back internally and reports the failure as a message row, updateUsingProcedure still answers “Success”. The user sees a green toast and nothing has changed. When a procedure can refuse the operation, call executeProcedureForMessageV1 instead — it surfaces the message.

Trap 2 — a read procedure must never return error_message

executeProcedureGetDataWithError treats a column called error_message as a thrown exception, which the API layer answers as HTTP 500. Several procedures were written to report “nothing found” that way. The result is a 500 on an empty search, which freezes RTK Query on stale data and looks like the UI ignored your filter. “Nothing matched” is a normal answer and must come back as zero rows.

Trap 3 — column aliases and BeanPropertyRowMapper

Spring lowercases a column label and matches it against the underscore form of the DTO property. underscoreName breaks on a case change and never before a digit. So categorizationTier1 must be aliased categorization_tier1, not categorization_tier_1. An alias that gets this wrong binds to nothing and yields a silent null — no error, no warning, just a blank column in the UI.

Paging

Paging is done inside the procedure with COUNT(*) OVER (), so a page and its true total arrive in one round trip and no separate *_Count procedure can drift out of sync with the WHERE clause. Page size is clamped inside the procedure. On the Java side, PaginationUtils and ProcedurePageResult<T> carry the result and PageResponseDto shapes the response.

Migrations

Schema and procedure changes live in airtelmanagement/db/migration/, one file per change, named YYYY-MM-DD_what-it-does.sql. There are around 45 of them. The conventions are strict and worth following exactly:

  • The date is when the change was authored, not when it was applied, and a file is never renamed afterwards.
  • Every file opens with a header comment stating what already existed and is left alone, what the file adds, why, and how to roll it back. 2026-09-04_ui_actions_audit_log.sql is the current reference for the shape.
  • Files are additive and re-runnable. Procedures are DROP PROCEDURE IF EXISTS then CREATE PROCEDURE; index creation is guarded against information_schema because MySQL has no CREATE INDEX IF NOT EXISTS. Running a migration twice must be a no-op, not an error.
Trap 4 — the live database and the repository drift

Procedures have been hand-edited on the server. It has happened more than once that the deployed procedure had extra parameters, missing columns or an entirely different body compared with the .sql file in git — in one case a deployed procedure had been silently truncated by eight columns.

Before you change any procedure, dump what is actually deployed and diff it against the file in db/migration/:

SHOW CREATE PROCEDURE Get_CRQ_Workflow_Overview;
SELECT ROUTINE_DEFINITION FROM information_schema.ROUTINES
 WHERE ROUTINE_NAME = 'Get_CRQ_Workflow_Overview';

When invoking the MySQL CLI on a procedure body, pass --comments — without it the client strips comments and you lose the header that explains the procedure.

Naming

Procedure names are not consistent, and you cannot infer behaviour from them. The clearest example, and one that has caused a production-shaped bug: SP_GET_MOP_DETAILS_BY_CRQN reads like a getter but inserts a MOP, a MOP version and a MOP file row, and refuses a second call. It must never be called from a GET handler. Read the body before you trust a name.

The single API instance

src/service/api.ts creates one createApi with an empty endpoints object and a long tagTypes list. Every feature then calls api.injectEndpoints(...). There are 37 such slices. The benefits are one cache, one middleware, one base query and cross-feature tag invalidation.

The base query does three things:

  1. Attaches Authorization: Bearer <token> from the auth slice.
  2. Sets credentials: "include" so the HttpOnly jwt cookie travels too — a second, XSS-resistant way to authenticate if the header is ever missing.
  3. Wraps the result: on 401 it clears storage, dispatches logout, resets the cache and toasts “session expired”; on 403 it toasts the server's message but leaves the session intact.
The auth-lifecycle exemption

Sign-in, logout and session-terminate are excluded from that handler. A 401 from /auth/v1/session/terminate means “wrong password”, not “your session expired” — the caller does not have a session yet, which is why they are on that screen. Without the exemption, a failed login would tear down state and toast a confusing message.

Authentication and Sessions

A JWT proves who you are; a database row proves your session is still live. Both must agree on every single request. Most login-related bugs in CHM's history come from forgetting the second half.

The two halves of a session

What must be true for a request to succeed
The JWTSigned with jwt.secret. Subject = numeric user id. jti = a UUID minted at login. Expiry seven days.
The session rowAUTH_JWT_TOKENS where token_id = that jti, valid = true and expires_at > NOW().
both checked on every request by JwtAuthenticationFilter
If either fails the response is 401 { "status": "Fail", "message": "Invalid session" } and the front end signs the user out.

Login

  1. AuthService.findPasswordByOlmId reads AUTH_CREDENTIAL for the username and verifies the password (or the configured master password).
  2. It checks for a live session with the shared LIVE_SESSION_PREDICATE (valid = true AND expires_at > NOW()) and returns Already Logged if one exists.
  3. Otherwise it deletes any previous row for the user and inserts a new one with a fresh token_id. Because the id is new every time, matching on it alone already accounts for logout and for re-login elsewhere.
  4. AuthController mints the JWT, sets the HttpOnly cookie, writes an explicit LOGIN audit row and returns accessToken.

The filter

JwtAuthenticationFilter runs before Spring Security's authorization check. Four behaviours deserve attention:

  • It skips public endpoints entirely. permitAll() does not stop a filter added with addFilterBefore from running, so a stale token left in a browser used to make even login itself fail with “Invalid session”. The fix was to share one list, PublicEndpoints.PATTERNS, between the filter's shouldNotFilter and SecurityConfig's matchers. Two hand-maintained copies had drifted.
  • /auth/v1/logout is deliberately not public. Ending a session requires proving whose it is. The olmId in the request body is accepted and ignored; invalidation keys off the token_id in the caller's own verified token.
  • It reads the token from the header or the cookie. Either is accepted.
  • Authorities are loaded lazily. The permission query only runs when the matched handler carries @PreAuthorize.
Simple access tokens

/usermanagement/v1/getaccesstoken issues a different kind of token whose subject is a raw username rather than a numeric id, with no session row behind it. These are a static integration credential. The filter recognises them (the subject does not match \d+), validates them separately, and refuses them on any handler carrying @PreAuthorize — they can never gain an authority.

Public endpoints

The complete list, from PublicEndpoints.PATTERNS:

/test/** /auth/v1/signin /auth/v1/session/terminate /actuator/** /users/signup /api/python/runScript /CHMDataAdapter/CreateCrq /usermanagement/v1/getaccesstoken

Adding an endpoint here makes it reachable from anywhere the service is. Do it only for machine-to-machine integrations, and put the list entry and the security reasoning in the same commit.

Front-end bootstrap: AuthHydrator

Mounted once in main.tsx, above the router. On a cold start it:

  1. Reads the token from localStorage. If there is none, it records the currently requested in-app path as a pending post-login redirect (the only place that is ever written), dispatches logout and finishes hydration.
  2. If there is one, it paints the cached user immediately so the UI does not blank out, then calls /users/v2/getloggeduserdetails to re-validate the token against the back end and refresh the permission tree. On failure it clears storage and logs out.
  3. Registers a storage listener for cross-tab sign-in and sign-out, and a pageshow listener so a bfcache restore (pressing Back after logout) re-validates instead of trusting the painted snapshot.
Why storage moved from session to local

The session used to live in sessionStorage, so a refresh or a new tab logged the user out. It is now localStorage, shared across tabs. That is only safe because the token is re-validated against the back end on every bootstrap — a cleared or expired session row cannot be skipped by a stale local copy.

Post-login redirect

postLoginRedirect is deliberately sessionStorage-scoped: it describes this tab's interrupted navigation and can never bleed into another. It is written in exactly one place, consumed once, and cleared by every session teardown — so a signed-out user's last page can never be handed to whoever signs in next on a shared machine. The value is also vetted through isPathAllowed() before being honoured.

Authorization: the RBAC Model

What a user can see is decided by data, not by code. There is exactly one file that maps URLs to required grants, and every guard in the app reads it.

The model

Role  ──►  Module  ──►  Sub-module  ──►  Permission
                                         VIEW · CREATE · UPDATE · DELETE · APPROVE · REJECT

A role is granted permissions on sub-modules. A module counts as granted when any of its sub-modules carries at least one permission. The grants live in the WEB_* tables and are edited at runtime through Global Settings › Admin Settings.

Sub-module names are data, and they contain typos

Gating is by exact name match against live rows. Under the Scheduler module the row really is spelled "Plan VIew And Setup", with that capital I.   3. getFirstAccessiblePath() — the per-user default landing page.
  4. In-page tab strips, which gate their tabs with the same predicate.

Because the same predicate serves all four, the sidebar and the route guard cannot disagree about a URL. routeAccess flattens parents and children, sorts by longest path first so a child wins over its parent, and treats an unregistered path as allowed — a safety net for a route added without a nav entry, rather than silently locking everyone out.

Four grant shapes

FieldEffect
requiredModuleModule-level gate. null means open to any signed-in user.
requiredSubModuleTightens to one sub-module of that module.
requiredAnyOfAllowed if any listed requirement is satisfied. My Dashboard uses this because it spans the “Dashboard” and “Me” modules.
requiredSuperAdminOnly a super admin, whatever modules they hold. Used by the Audit Log so that access cannot be delegated from Global Settings.

Route-only entries

ROUTE_ONLY_ACCESS_ENTRIES covers screens that are guarded but not shown in the sidebar — today, /user-management/auditlog. Without it, that path would prefix-match the plain /user-management entry and open to anyone holding the User Management module.

Two deliberate module-level fallbacks

MODULE_ONLY_GUARD downgrades sub-module enforcement to module level for Cab Manager and SFTP Management. In both cases the live sub-module rows do not match the names in the registry — CAB falls back to a persona table when a user has no sub-module rows, and SFTP's live rows are named Dashboard/Files/Servers rather than Windows/Linux. Enforcing them at the route layer would lock real users out of every page in those modules.

A gating bug worth learning from

The Scheduler children once declared requiredModule: "Role-Based Access Control" — the admin RBAC module, copy-pasted rather than intended. The effect was that a Scheduler page only appeared for someone holding both Scheduler and admin RBAC, so every role granted Scheduler alone saw the parent item with no children and could never reach CRQ Workflow. The identical bug hit Inbox, which was gated on “Notification System” (the admin-side config module) instead of “Inbox”. When a page is invisible for a role that should have it, suspect the module name first.

Enforcement is three-deep

  1. Sidebar — the item is not rendered. Convenience, not security.
  2. Route guard — PrivateRoute refuses the pathname and renders AccessDenied.
  3. Back end — @PreAuthorize, or an explicit check inside the service, refuses the data. The audit log additionally has a procedure, sp_get_ui_actions_log_access, that answers the same question server-side.

A change to visibility that only touches layer one is not a security change. Say so in your PR and make sure layer three exists.

Organisation scoping is separate from RBAC

Permissions decide which screens; the organisation hierarchy decides which rows. The Vertical / Function / Domain / Sub-domain pickers at the top of most screens are derived from the user's Organization Hierarchy grants, with a legacy visibility table as fallback. Two similarly named things are not the same taxonomy: an organisation domain_id and the chm_domain on a plan are different trees. Confusing them yields empty screens with no error.

The table page, the action dialog, the card, the hooks and the API slice are all driven off that map. Adding a stage is:

  1. Add its key to StageKey in types/stageWorkflow.types.ts.
  2. Add one entry to STAGE_CONFIG_MAP.
  3. Optionally override fields or statusOptions if its form differs.

Plan & Inventory is the exception. It predates the generic map and runs on its own endpoints (crqreviewApiSlice, schedulerApiSlice), which is why its stage id is "review" and it is not a StageKey.

Every stage's action shape

Three verbs per stage: /start, /pause, /done. The outcome options are shared — Pass (Done), Failed, and Cancelled — and choosing Cancelled reveals a shared cancellation block: which team to send the activity back to, the Remedy status, a reason, a derived rollback owner and a Remedy remark. That block is declared once in constants/sharedFields.ts and spread into every stage.

Refusals are first-class

Update_CRQ_Scheduling_To_Done_Or_Failed has five branches that refuse the transition. Those are surfaced as proper 409/404 responses via StageActionBlockedException and rendered as an inline alert in the dialog, rather than a generic failure toast. When you add a guard to a stage procedure, wire it the same way — the user needs to know which precondition failed.

The blank-shift key bug — a Jackson naming trap

Rule of thumb for auditing your own feature

Annotate the endpoints that change state. Do not annotate a GET that merely renders a screen — opening a page is not a user action. Do annotate a GET that hands the user a document; that is a DOWNLOAD.

Conventions

Most of these are enforced by nothing except review. They are still worth following exactly, because every one of them exists because breaking it cost somebody a day.

Back end

  • Package by feature, then controller / service / dto. A new functional area gets its own top-level package, not a folder inside an existing one.
  • Services extend BaseService and use databaseUtils. Do not autowire a JdbcTemplate yourself.
  • No SQL in feature services. A new read path is a new procedure and a new migration file.
  • Controllers return ResponseEntity, and refusals use the typed exceptions so GlobalExceptionHandler produces a consistent body.
  • DTOs are Lombok classes or records. Field names must match the underscore form of the procedure's column aliases (Chapter 10).
  • Version an endpoint rather than change its shape when other callers exist — the codebase already has /v1, /v2, /v3 variants for exactly this reason.

Front end

  • One folder per feature, with the internal layout from Chapter 11. Export through index.ts; import other features only through their barrel.
  • Server state belongs to RTK Query, not to a slice. Add a slice only for genuinely local UI state that several components share.
  • Never create a second createApi. Always api.injectEndpoints.
  • Declare tags in service/api.ts and match the existing spelling exactly, typos included.
  • Register a new route in the nav registry even if it should not appear in the sidebar — use ROUTE_ONLY_ACCESS_ENTRIES. An unregistered path is allowed by the guard.
  • Lazy-load route elements, and remember that a heavy library still lands in the vendor chunk unless manualChunks splits it.
  • TypeScript, no any in new code. The existing code has some; do not add more.
The comment style, and why it is unusual

This codebase carries long explanatory comments where a decision is not obvious — often describing the bug that motivated the current shape. That is deliberate and it is the house style. When you fix something subtle, leave the why behind, not a restatement of the what. Several chapters of this handbook were reconstructed from exactly those comments.

Database migrations

Recapping Chapter 10 as a checklist, because this is where review is strictest:

  • File is db/migration/YYYY-MM-DD_what-it-does.sql, dated when authored.
  • Header comment states what already existed, what this adds, why, and how to roll back.
  • DROP PROCEDURE IF EXISTS before CREATE; index creation guarded via information_schema. Running it twice is a no-op.
  • Column aliases are the underscore form of the DTO field, with no underscore before a digit.
  • A read procedure returns zero rows for “nothing found”, never an error_message column.
  • Paging is COUNT(*) OVER () in the same query, page size clamped inside.
  • You dumped the deployed procedure and diffed it before editing.

Git

  • Work on master-main; pull requests target main.
  • Commit messages are short and feature-shaped — “Add MOP validation workspace and PDF viewer”.
  • Ship the migration file in the same commit as the code that needs it. There is no separate change process for SQL.
  • Remember the auto-commit watcher (Chapter 2): check git log, not git status, and re-check before assuming a change is unpushed.

Walkthrough: Adding a Feature End to End

A worked example. The task: add a screen listing every CRQ that has been paused for more than seven days, gated behind the Scheduler module. Six steps, three layers.

Step 1 — the procedure

New read path, so: new procedure, new migration file. Create db/migration/2026-09-08_stalled_crq_report.sql:

-- ============================================================================
-- Stalled CRQ report
-- Date   : 2026-09-08
-- Target : Vegayan_CHM (DBSOURCE1)
--
-- Existing and untouched: CRQ_MASTER_TBL, CRQ_STAGE_ASSIGN_TBL.
-- Adds    : Get_Stalled_CRQs — one paged read of CRQs sitting in ON_HOLD
--           for more than :p_days, scoped to a domain / sub-domain.
-- Why     : the CRQ Workflow list cannot express "paused for N days" and
--           planners were exporting to a spreadsheet to find them.
-- Rollback: DROP PROCEDURE IF EXISTS Get_Stalled_CRQs;
-- Re-runnable: yes (drop + create).
-- ============================================================================
DROP PROCEDURE IF EXISTS Get_Stalled_CRQs;
DELIMITER $$
CREATE PROCEDURE Get_Stalled_CRQs(
    IN p_domain_id     INT,
    IN p_sub_domain_id VARCHAR(10),
    IN p_days          INT,
    IN p_page          INT,
    IN p_size          INT
)
BEGIN
    DECLARE v_size INT DEFAULT LEAST(GREATEST(IFNULL(p_size, 25), 1), 200);  -- clamp
    DECLARE v_off  INT DEFAULT GREATEST(IFNULL(p_page, 0), 0) * v_size;

    SELECT
        m.crq_no                     AS crq_no,
        m.crq_id                     AS crq_id,
        m.current_stage              AS current_stage,
        m.current_status             AS current_status,
        sa.stage_start_date          AS paused_since,
        DATEDIFF(NOW(), sa.stage_start_date) AS paused_days,
        COUNT(*) OVER ()             AS total_count          -- page + true total, one trip
    FROM CRQ_MASTER_TBL m
    JOIN CRQ_STAGE_ASSIGN_TBL sa
      ON sa.crq_id = m.crq_id AND sa.stage = m.current_stage
    WHERE m.current_status = 'ON_HOLD'
      AND m.domain_id      = p_domain_id
      AND m.sub_domain_id  = p_sub_domain_id
      AND DATEDIFF(NOW(), sa.stage_start_date) > IFNULL(p_days, 7)
    ORDER BY paused_days DESC
    LIMIT v_size OFFSET v_off;
END$$
DELIMITER ;

Note the three conventions in play: no error_message column (an empty result is simply no rows), COUNT(*) OVER () for paging, and aliases that will bind cleanly to crqNo, pausedSince, pausedDays.

Verify in this order

  • Nothing was audited — correct for a read.
  • Nothing invalidates the new tag yet. If a stage action should refresh this list, add the tag to that mutation's invalidatesTags.
  • The procedure is in the repository but not in the live database until somebody runs it. Say so explicitly in the PR description; this is the single most common cause of “works on my machine” in CHM.

Pitfalls and Troubleshooting

Every entry here is a real failure that has happened on this project, and every one of them initially looked like a different problem. Read the symptom column first.

Build and startup

Hundreds of “cannot find symbol” errors on getters and setters

Cause: not JDK 17. Lombok's annotation processing stops running and every @Data class loses its accessors.
Fix: point JAVA_HOME at JDK 17 and set the same SDK in IntelliJ's project settings and Maven runner.

The back end will not start and the stack trace mentions a properties file

Cause: the external config file is missing or unreadable at the hard-coded path.
Fix: create C:\vegayan\simplus\airtelcms-config.properties from the sample in deployment/config/. See Chapter 5.

The build fails on committed merge-conflict markers

It has happened that <<<<<<< markers were committed into several source files, breaking every build until removed. If a fresh clone will not compile, grep for conflict markers before debugging anything else.

Authentication

Every request answers “Invalid session”

Cause: the AUTH_JWT_TOKENS row for this token's jti is gone, has valid = false, or has expired. It is never a malformed JWT — a bad signature or expiry fails earlier with a different message.
Fix: sign in again. If it recurs immediately, check whether something else is logging that account out: a second session elsewhere, or the cleanup job with a stale expires_at.

Login itself fails with “Invalid session”

Cause: historically, a stale token in the browser was validated by the JWT filter even on public routes, because permitAll() does not skip a filter. Fixed by sharing PublicEndpoints.PATTERNS.
If you see it again: you have probably added a public endpoint to SecurityConfig only. Add it to PublicEndpoints instead — both mechanisms read that one list.

“Already Logged” and you cannot get in

Use the terminate-session flow on the login screen (it re-checks your password), or delete the account's row from AUTH_JWT_TOKENS.

Data and procedures

A column is always null in the UI but correct in the database

Cause: the column alias does not match what BeanPropertyRowMapper derives from the DTO field. The classic case is a digit: categorizationTier1 maps to categorization_tier1, never categorization_tier_1.
Fix: correct the alias in the procedure. There will be no error to guide you — only the null.

A search with no matches returns HTTP 500 and the screen freezes on old data

Cause: the read procedure reports “nothing found” as an error_message row, which DatabaseUtils turns into an exception and the API layer into a 500. RTK Query keeps the previous successful result on screen.
Fix: change the procedure to return zero rows. As a defensive measure, callers can catch a cause-less DatabaseOperationException.

A save reports success and nothing changes

Cause: either updateUsingProcedure was used on a procedure that can roll back, or the procedure has an empty SQLEXCEPTION handler that swallows the error and returns a success row. Both have happened.
Fix: use executeProcedureForMessageV1, and make the procedure report its own failure.

A procedure call fails with the wrong number of arguments

Cause: live/repository drift. Deployed procedures have gained and lost parameters without the .sql file changing.
Fix: SHOW CREATE PROCEDURE first, always.

A multi-result-set procedure suddenly returns the wrong data

Cause: row order or column names in sp_get_crq_journey_page have drifted twice already.
Fix: resolve rows by name, never by index.

Front end

“Invalid hook call” or “Cannot read properties of null (reading 'useContext')” in dev

Cause: two copies of React alive in one tab, caused by Vite's dependency optimiser re-running mid-session and bumping the shared browserHash. The stack trace shows two different ?v= hashes. It is never a Rules-of-Hooks violation in your component.
Fix: stop the dev server, delete node_modules/.vite, restart, and do a hard reload of the tab. The resolve.dedupe and optimizeDeps.include entries in vite.config.ts exist to prevent this; do not remove them.

“Cannot access X before initialization” in a production build only

Cause: React, MUI and Emotion split into separate chunk files. Their circular internal references mean one chunk executes before another has finished initialising its exports.
Fix: keep them in the single vendor chunk. This is why manualChunks looks so blunt.

A lazy-loaded library still loads on app start

Cause: everything under node_modules goes to vendor, so React.lazy alone defers nothing.
Fix: give the library its own manualChunks branch, anchored on the package directory. Beware substring matching — "react-pdf" as a substring also catches @react-pdf/renderer, a different library.

Requests fail in the browser but work in curl

Cause: CORS. Your dev origin is not in the allow-list.
Fix: add it to SecurityConfig.corsConfigurationSource().

A page is invisible for a role that should have it

Cause, in order of likelihood: the nav entry names the wrong module; it names a sub-module row that does not exist; the grant genuinely is not there.
Fix: compare the registry string against the live WEB_MODULE / WEB_SUB_MODULE rows, character for character.

A fit-to-container component flickers at certain window sizes

Cause: only html has scrollbar-gutter: stable, so a component that sizes itself to its container can oscillate across the scrollbar boundary — content fits, scrollbar disappears, content no longer fits, and round again.
Fix: write fit logic with hysteresis, or reserve the gutter on the container.

Verification without credentials

Two harnesses the team has used

When you need to check RBAC behaviour or take a screenshot but have no working login: build a small esbuild harness that imports the real rbac/ modules and exercises the predicates directly; or drive the app with Playwright, intercepting getloggeduserdetails with a stubbed permission payload and seeding localStorage. Both avoid inventing a fake login path in the product.

The Nginx site

server {
    listen 80 default_server;
    root   /var/www/chm;

    client_max_body_size 1024m;   # Excel uploads, CRQ attachments, SFTP transfers
    proxy_read_timeout   300s;    # the backend triggers slow SSH/SFTP jobs

    location /airtelchmbeta/      { try_files $uri $uri/ /airtelchmbeta/index.html; }
    location /changemanagementnew/ { proxy_pass http://127.0.0.1:1857/; }
    location = /                  { return 302 /airtelchmbeta/; }
}

Three details matter. The SPA fallback to index.html is what makes a deep link survive a refresh. The large body size and long read timeout exist because real uploads and remote scripts are slow. Actuator on 8686 is deliberately not proxied.

Retargeting the base path

The SPA lives under a sub-path. To move it, change VITE_APP_BASE_PATH in .env.production, rebuild, and update the matching Nginx location blocks. Never edit source: Vite's base and BrowserRouter's basename both derive from that one variable, so they cannot drift.

Linux specifics

Some Windows paths are baked into the WAR and must be overridden at launch — no rebuild needed:

java -jar /opt/chm/app/airtelmanagement.war \
     --file.base.path=/opt/chm/files_path \
     --file.base.path2=/opt/chm/files_path2 \
     --logging.config=/opt/chm/config/logback-linux.xml \
     --logging.level.com.vegayan.airtelmanagement=INFO

The last flag matters: application.properties ships several packages at DEBUG, which is far too noisy for production.

The external config file must exist at /home/vegayan/simplus/config_airtel.properties, and /home/vegayan/simplus/sftp_uploads/ is a hard-coded working directory. The deployment/scripts/linux/ folder carries build.sh, deploy.sh, start/stop/restart scripts, a systemd unit and a logrotate config; deployment/scripts/windows/ has the equivalent .bat files and a service installer.

Verification after a deploy

  • curl http://127.0.0.1:8686/actuator/health returns UP on the server.
  • The login page loads at the base path and a real account can sign in.
  • A deep link (for example /airtelchmbeta/roster/view) survives a browser refresh — this proves the SPA fallback.
  • A file upload of realistic size completes — this proves the body-size and timeout settings.
  • Any migration shipped in this release has actually been applied to the target database.
Migrations are not applied by deployment

Nothing in the build or start-up applies db/migration/*.sql. A release that adds or changes a procedure requires somebody to run that file against the target database. Make it an explicit line item in the release notes.

HTTPS

The deployment package includes HTTPS-MIGRATION.md and a reference chm-airtel-https.conf. The application itself needs no change — the JWT is stateless and travels in a header, so there is no sticky-session or cookie-path handling to revisit. HSTS should be added only once HTTPS is actually in place.

First-Week Checklist

If you can tick everything here by Friday, you are ready to pick up a real ticket.

Day 1 — running

  • Both repositories cloned under D:\CHM_airtel_scheduler\Github_Repo\.
  • java -version reports 17. JAVA_HOME and IntelliJ agree.
  • External config file created; the back end starts and /actuator/health is UP.
  • .env.local written; npm run chalu-hoja serves the login page.
  • You can sign in, and you know how to clear an “Already Logged” session.

Day 2 — orientation

  • Visited every screen your role can open; you can name the module behind each.
  • Read src/rbac/navRegistry.tsx top to bottom.
  • Read src/service/api.ts and understand the 401/403 handling.
  • Opened a CRQ and traced its seven stages in the UI.
  • Read db/README.md.

Day 3 — tracing

  • Picked one screen and followed a request from the browser network tab to the controller, to the service, to the CALL in the SQL log.
  • Ran that CALL yourself in a MySQL client and matched the output to the screen.
  • Dumped one deployed procedure with SHOW CREATE PROCEDURE and diffed it against its migration file.
  • Found where that screen's route, nav entry and permission gate are declared.

Day 4 — a small change

  • Made a visible change to one screen and saw it hot-reload.
  • Made a back-end change, restarted, and confirmed it — remembering there is no hot reload.
  • Added a field end to end: procedure alias → DTO → TypeScript type → UI.
  • Confirmed the alias binds (no silent null).

Day 5 — understanding the traps

  • You can explain why “Invalid session” is always about a database row.
  • You can explain why a read procedure must not return error_message.
  • You can explain why updateUsingProcedure can report a false success.
  • You can explain why the live database and db/migration/ may disagree, and what to do about it.
  • You know that hiding a sidebar item is not a security boundary.
  • You know that a migration in the repository has not been applied to any database until somebody runs it.

Questions worth asking your team

  • Which database am I pointed at, and who else is using it?
  • Which role code am I signed in as, and which one should I test against?
  • Which migrations in db/migration/ are not applied to my environment?
  • Is the Cygnet sync meant to be on in this environment?
  • Who owns the Remedy integration credentials?
One last piece of advice

When something in CHM behaves oddly, the answer is usually in one of three places: the SQL log (what was actually asked of the database), the live procedure body (what actually ran), or the nav registry (what the user was actually allowed to see). Check those three before you reach for a debugger.
